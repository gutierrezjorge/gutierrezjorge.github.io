I am a biologist studying how organisms physically modify their environment, that is, how they act as ecosystem engineers, and what this means for biodiversity, ecosystem functioning and the integrity of the physical environment. I work mainly in coastal environments of the Southwestern Atlantic, from rocky shores and coralline algal beds to salt marshes and dunes. I received my PhD in Biological Sciences from the Universidad Nacional de Mar del Plata, Argentina, and did my postdoctoral work at the Institute of Ecosystem Studies (now the Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies) in the United States. In my free time I enjoy birdwatching and share my sightings on eBird.

Research

Environmental Modification by Organisms

My research focuses on organisms as ecosystem engineers, that is, on how organisms physically modify their environment. I study the consequences of these modifications for the composition and richness of biological communities, for the flows of materials and energy in ecosystems, and for the integrity of the physical environment through processes such as bioerosion and bioprotection. I also investigate how the physical environment and other organisms influence the abundance, persistence and activity of engineers, as well as various aspects of their biology. My interest in this topic arose early in my career, while I was studying the shell beds of the stout razor clam Tagelus plebeius as distinctive benthic habitats in an Argentinean coastal lagoon (Mar Chiquita). Since then, I have worked on a variety of study systems and contributed to the conceptual development of ecosystem engineering, its application across taxonomic groups and environments, and its integration with, and distinction from, other ecological concepts.

  1. Gutiérrez JL, Iribarne OO (1999). Role of Holocene beds of the stout razor clam Tagelus plebeius in structuring present benthic communities. Marine Ecology Progress Series 185: 213-228.

Why study ecosystem engineers?

Often, the strongest effect of a species on others is not eating them, being eaten by them or competing with them, but changing the place where they live. By physically modifying the environment, ecosystem engineers determine which species can live in a place, regulate flows of materials and energy and affect the stability of the physical environment itself, often with effects that outlast the engineers themselves. Understanding them is key to explaining community structure and ecosystem functioning. It is also essential for conserving and restoring habitats that depend on them, such as salt marshes, seagrass meadows, mangrove and macroalgal forests, and invertebrate reefs and beds, and for anticipating the effects of biological invasions and climate change.

Rocky intertidal shores

Mussel beds

On rocky shores of the Southwestern Atlantic, mussels such as Brachidontes rodriguezii and Perumytilus purpuratus form dense beds that retain water and sediment and provide refuge for a wide diversity of invertebrates. Together with Gabriela Palomo (MACN, CONICET) and our students, we have been investigating how these beds, along with the algae and other epibionts growing on them, modify physical conditions, support associated communities and protect soft rock platforms from erosion. We also investigate which physical and biological factors regulate their distribution, persistence and recovery after disturbance (wave action, heat waves, attachment strength).

Shallow reefs · Brazil and Argentina

Coralline algae

Coralline algae build calcareous structures that modify the physical environment and provide habitat for many organisms. Together with Guilherme Pereira-Filho (UNIFESP) and other Brazilian colleagues and students, we have been studying rhodolith beds (free-living nodules of coralline algae): how they support invertebrate and fish diversity, how their habitat function persists even after algal death, and how other organisms, such as mound-building fishes, modify them. Together with colleagues from Brazil and Argentina, we are currently extending our interests to the role of articulated (geniculate) coralline algae in the biodiversity of Southwestern Atlantic shallow reefs and their response to climate extremes.

Intertidal platforms · bioerosion

Boring bivalves

My work on this system originated from an initiative by María Bagur (CADIC, CONICET) and focused mainly on the date mussel Leiosolenus patagonicus, which chemically bores into the carbonate rocks of intertidal platforms and is one of the main agents of coastal bioerosion. We studied its biology (growth, age, mortality), the bioerosion rates it generates, and the calcium carbonate balance between what it dissolves when boring and what it deposits in its shell and in the calcareous lining of its boreholes. We also investigated how vacant boreholes serve as refuges and maintain invertebrate species richness in physically harsh environments.

Pampean coastal dunes

Dune plants

In Pampean coastal dunes, mats of the invasive iceplant Carpobrotus cover large areas and change environmental conditions at ground level. In recent years I have studied how these mats create thermal refugia that allow the invasive land snail Theba pisana to survive the summer. This increases the snail's abundance and its impact on native vegetation, a case of facilitation between invasive species. I am currently involved in global-scale studies on the ecology, genetics and morphology of the Carpobrotus hybrid complex.

Southwestern Atlantic salt marshes

Salt marshes: plants and crabs

In Southwestern Atlantic salt marshes, plants such as Spartina densiflora produce large amounts of detritus, and the burrowing crab Neohelice granulata reworks sediment and builds dense burrow networks. Under the mentorship of Clive Jones (Cary Institute) and Oscar Iribarne (UNMdP), I studied how crab activity modifies carbon availability in the sediment and the accumulation of plant litter on the marsh surface. I also studied how this activity affects litter export to adjacent environments and, thereby, marsh functioning and connectivity.

Synthesis and theory

Conceptual development

Together with Clive G. Jones and colleagues from several countries, I have developed conceptual frameworks to understand how the physical modification of the environment by organisms affects communities and ecosystem functioning. This work includes ecosystem engineering as a source of biogeochemical heterogeneity, its application to different groups (such as shell-producing mollusks) and coastal environments, and its links with other fields, such as the study of biological invasions.

Teaching

Organisms as Ecosystem Engineers

Since 2019 I have taught the graduate course “Organisms as Ecosystem Engineers” at the Facultad de Ciencias Exactas y Naturales, Universidad Nacional de Mar del Plata, with the collaboration of María Bagur (CADIC, CONICET) since 2020. The course introduces the ecosystem engineering concept, its consequences for communities and ecosystems, and its applications in conservation and restoration.

2019 – present

History of the course

A precursor of the course was the 2005 edition taught by Clive G. Jones in Mar del Plata, in which I participated as a collaborator. The course I have taught since 2019 is a renewed and continually updated version, since in the intervening years the development of the ecosystem engineering concept, its usage and its applications have advanced enormously, and they continue to advance today. In 2023 I also taught a marine version of the course, with a fieldwork component, for the Programa de Pós-Graduação em Biodiversidade e Ecologia Marinha e Costeira at the Instituto do Mar (UNIFESP), held in Picinguaba, Ubatuba (SP), Brazil.

Syllabus

Contents

The course is organized in six units: introduction, formal definition of the concept, biotic consequences of ecosystem engineering, how to study engineers, their uses and services, and their restoration. It closes with student presentations.

Students

Where students come from

So far, more than 110 students have completed the course at UNMdP and its marine version at UNIFESP. They come from across Argentina, from Catamarca to Ushuaia, as well as from Brazil, the United States and Costa Rica.
